[ TITLE ]
Scroll SVG Scenery Around a Centered Subject

[ PURPOSE ]
Create continuous scrolling scenery with correct tile seams, coverage and relative motion.

STATUS: PILOT MODULE. It can be used for scenery alone. A cat is optional. When a walking figure
is present, this module owns scenery motion; the figure's articulation belongs to its rig module.

[ ALGORITHM ]
1. Declare the camera intent. In SUPPORT_MOVES mode the cat stays approximately fixed in screen x
   while support landmarks move in the opposite direction to its implied travel. Vertical bob is
   independent. Avoid also translating the cat unless the relative-speed contract explicitly says so.

2. Establish the true visual repeat distance D, including posts, wood grain, shadows and variations.
   Move a tiled strip from 0 to -D over period T for rightward apparent travel. Speed magnitude=D/T.
   A multiple of a complete tile is also valid. One post spacing is insufficient if decorations
   repeat only every several posts. Endpoint geometry and velocity should match at a smooth seam.

3. Prebuild enough repeated geometry on both sides to cover the entire viewport for every scroll
   offset. Use reusable defs/use when appropriate. Inspect both canvas edges immediately before
   and after reset; do not generate a needed post only after an empty region has appeared.

4. A continuous, horizontally invariant rail can remain stationary while periodic posts scroll;
   a rail with knots, highlights or textures that identify a position must share the intended
   motion or have its own matching repeat. This is a visual camera construction, not a requirement
   that every support element have a nonzero transform.

5. For a centered walker, stance paws travel backward at approximately the support's screen
   velocity; swing paws lift and recover forward. Couple gait to relative speed. Changing stride
   duration without reconsidering scroll speed can introduce sliding. Validate contact independently.

6. Give background depth layers smaller apparent speeds when that matches the camera design.
   Example B: fence 70/1.2=58.333 units/s; hedge 196/4.2=46.667 units/s. Each has its own tile width
   and reset clock. Different periods alone do not establish slower motion or convincing parallax.

7. Test each seam on its own clock, then the assembled scene. For a gait comparison, compare
   different limb phases while controlling or excluding unrelated motion. In example B a delta
   of 8.4s aligns fence and hedge but not its 0.45s bob or 0.9s gait. A changed cat crop therefore
   does not by itself isolate the legs. A delta of 0.45s aligns bob and advances gait half a cycle,
   but does not align scenery: use a suitable mask, limb transforms, or an explicitly labeled
   diagnostic copy with unrelated tracks frozen. Preserve the original.

[ EXAMPLES ]
[Setup]: A fence has identical pickets every 70 units and scrolls -70 units each 1.2 seconds.
[Process]: Repeat pickets beyond the viewport and inspect t=1.199 and t=1.2, with the clock verified.
[Result]: The reset can be visually continuous even though the strip's translation jumps back to zero.

[ SELF_CHECK ]
Q1: Does a stationary cat root mean the scene cannot show walking?
A1: No. Inspect the gait and moving support landmarks relative to the cat.
Q2: Is a 4.2-second hedge always slower than a 1.2-second fence?
A2: Not from periods alone; divide each displacement by its period.
Q3: Does an 8.4-second screenshot difference prove leg movement in example B?
A3: No. Bob also changes phase; isolate the limb evidence.
